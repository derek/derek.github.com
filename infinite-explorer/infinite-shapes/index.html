Strange Shapes

Three shapes built by repeating a simple step forever. Each ends up with a property that sounds impossible.

The Cantor set: all gone, yet uncountably many left

Start with the line from 0 to 1. Remove the open middle third. You have two pieces. Remove the middle third of each. Now four pieces. Keep going forever.

Each step keeps only 2/3 of the length, so after n steps the total length left is (2/3)n, which shrinks toward zero. The finished set has zero length, so it's all gaps. And yet what's left isn't empty. The endpoints 0, 1/3, 2/3, 1, 1/9, … survive forever. More than that, there are uncountably many points left, as many as in the entire original line.

Pick a surviving point

At each step, choose the left (L) or right (R) piece. Your sequence of choices is the point's address. Tap to flip a choice.

Why uncountably many? Each point is pinned down by an infinite string of left/right choices, and that's exactly an infinite binary sequence from the diagonal page. Different strings give different points, and we know those can't be listed. Written in base 3, the points of the Cantor set are precisely the numbers between 0 and 1 whose digits are all 0s and 2s: left is 0, right is 2.

The Hilbert curve: a line that fills a square

Draw a path that visits every cell of a 2 × 2 grid. Then a 4 × 4 grid, in a way that refines the previous path. Then 8 × 8, and so on. The recipe is simple and recursive. The limit of these paths is a single continuous curve that passes through every point of the square.

A one-dimensional line covering a two-dimensional area breaks the idea that curves are "thin". Peano discovered such a curve in 1890, and Hilbert gave a simpler one in 1891.

Hilbert curve, step by step

Watch the length. At order n the curve is about 2n long (for a square with side 1), so the paths get longer and longer, while the limit fills a square whose side is 1. The color runs from the start of the path to the end, so you can see that each part of the path stays within its own part of the square, which is why the limit is well-defined.

One caveat: the curve is not one-to-one. It has to pass through some points more than once. A continuous, non-overlapping curve onto the whole square would be a perfect match between a segment and a square. Remove one interior point from a segment and it falls into two pieces; remove one point from a square and it stays in one piece. So that match can't exist.

Gabriel's horn: fill it, but you can't paint it

Take the curve y = 1/x for x ≥ 1 and spin it around the x-axis. You get an infinitely long horn that gets thinner and thinner.

Its volume is finite, exactly π. Its surface area is infinite. The surface needs more and more material for each stretch you add, in the same way the harmonic series did on the last page. So you could fill the horn with π cubic units of paint, but would need infinitely much paint to cover the outside.

Cut the horn off at x = t

The standard way out of the "paradox" is that the surface area counts a layer of paint with zero thickness. Real paint has some thickness, and the horn eventually gets narrower than that thickness. Out where the horn is thinner than a molecule, the "paint the outside" job is physically meaningless, while the mathematical area is still infinite.

Go deeper: the integrals

Spinning y = 1/x about the x-axis from 1 to t:

V(t) = π ∫1t x−2 dx = π(1 − 1/t) → π

A(t) = 2π ∫1t (1/x)√(1 + x−4) dx ≥ 2π ∫1t dx/x = 2π ln t → ∞

The Cantor set: write x = Σ ci/3i with each ci ∈ {0, 2}. The map from binary sequences (di) to Σ 2di/3i is a bijection onto the set, so it has cardinality 2ℵ₀, the same as the real line. Its length (Lebesgue measure) is lim (2/3)n = 0.

The Hilbert curve is the uniform limit of continuous maps hn : [0,1] → [0,1]², so the limit is a continuous map from [0,1] onto [0,1]².
