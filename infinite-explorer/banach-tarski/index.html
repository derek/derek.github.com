3 · The paradox

Banach–Tarski

Cut a solid ball into a handful of pieces, slide and rotate the pieces without stretching or bending them, and put them back together as two balls, each exactly the size of the original.

The theorem (Banach and Tarski, 1924). A solid ball in three-dimensional space can be split into finitely many pieces that can be moved by rotations and translations only, to form two solid balls, each congruent to the first. Five pieces are enough.

This is a real theorem, not a trick of wording. It also doesn't mean you can duplicate gold, and the reasons why are the most interesting part. We'll get there in three steps: what the "pieces" actually are, the infinite tree that makes the trick possible, and why physics isn't threatened.

What kind of "pieces"?

Forget knives. The pieces are not blobs you could ever carve out. Each one is an infinitely scattered cloud of points, arranged so intricately that it has no meaningful volume at all. (Mathematicians call such sets non-measurable.)

That's the escape hatch for the "volume doubled" worry: volume is only defined for well-behaved sets, and these pieces aren't. You can't add up volumes you don't have.

The pieces can only be proved to exist using the axiom of choice, the principle from the last page that lets you pick one item from each of infinitely many sets. Nobody can write down a rule that produces them.

The trick: a tree that is secretly two trees

The whole paradox comes from one fact about a simple infinite structure. Picture a city where, at every intersection, you can walk one of four ways: a (right), a⁻¹ (left, undoing a), b (up) and b⁻¹ (down, undoing b). Consider every route that never directly backtracks. Each route is a different point of an infinite branching tree, drawn below. (Only the first five levels fit on screen. The real tree never stops.)

Sort the routes by their first step, and the tree falls into four arms plus the starting point.

Now the key move. Take every route in the pink a⁻¹ arm and put an extra a step in front of it. The first step of each route was a⁻¹, so the new a cancels it, and the route just loses its first step. What does that make? Every route that doesn't start with a. So the pink arm, after one step, grows to fill the whole tree except the amber arm.

At the end of the slider, each of the two small pictures is a complete tree. The same is true of the green and blue arms with an extra b. We started with one tree. We cut it into four arms (plus one lonely point). Then we moved only two arms to make one full tree, and moved the other two to make another full tree. One tree became two, with nothing stretched and nothing added.

(The drawing only goes five levels deep, so the outermost ring of the real tree is missing from the copies. In the infinite tree there is no outermost ring, which is exactly why this works. In a finite tree the arms could never cover everything.)

From a tree to a ball

The remaining job is to find that tree inside a sphere. Here is the sketch:

  1. Find two rotations that act like a and b. There are rotations of 3D space (for example turning by the angle arccos(1/3) about two perpendicular axes) such that no sequence of them ever cancels out except in the obvious way. Their possible sequences form exactly the tree above.
  2. Chop the sphere's surface into orbits. Take a point and apply every possible sequence of those rotations. The points you reach are its orbit. Every point of the sphere lies in exactly one orbit, and almost every orbit looks like a copy of the tree.
  3. Choose a starting point in each orbit. There are uncountably many orbits, so there's no rule for it. This is the step that uses the axiom of choice.
  4. Cut and rearrange. The four arms of the tree become pieces of the sphere. Applying a to the "pink" piece and b to the "blue" piece does to the sphere what the slider did to the tree: the moved pieces now cover the sphere twice over.
  5. Tidy up the leftovers. A countable set of points (where the rotation axes pierce the sphere) misbehaves, and a clever extra rotation patches that. Then extend radially inward from the surface, and treat the exact centre separately, and you have a solid ball.

Why your orange is safe

  • Real matter isn't a continuum of points. A gold bar is a finite number of atoms. The construction cuts at scales far below any atom, into pieces with no volume.
  • You can't actually perform the cut. The pieces need the axiom of choice. They cannot be described, drawn or computed.
  • It's dimension-specific. In a line or on a flat plane, this kind of doubling is impossible: Banach showed there is a well-behaved "size" that every subset has, so volumes can't mysteriously double. The difference in 3D is that its rotations are tangled enough to contain the tree above.

The upshot is not "mathematics is broken". It's that "every set of points has a volume" cannot be true if you also accept the axiom of choice. Most mathematicians keep choice and give up that intuition. Others work in set theories with weaker choice principles, where every set of points does have a volume and Banach–Tarski fails.

Go deeper: the exact statements

Let F be the free group on generators a, b (the tree above). Write W(x) for the set of reduced words that begin with the letter x. Then

F = {e} ⊔ W(a) ⊔ W(a⁻¹) ⊔ W(b) ⊔ W(b⁻¹)

and the identity that does all the work is, for left-multiplication,

a · W(a⁻¹) = F ∖ W(a), hence F = W(a) ⊔ a·W(a⁻¹), and likewise F = W(b) ⊔ b·W(b⁻¹).

A group (or a set acted on by a group) with a decomposition like this is called paradoxical. Hausdorff (1914) showed that SO(3), the rotation group of 3-space, contains a copy of F, and used it to prove a paradox for the sphere minus a countable set. Banach and Tarski (1924) extended this to the solid ball. Raphael Robinson (1947) showed five pieces suffice for turning one ball into two, and that fewer won't do.

A stronger form: any two bounded subsets of ℝ³ with non-empty interior are equidecomposable, so a pea can be cut up and reassembled into the Sun.

The paradox needs only a weak form of choice. In dimensions 1 and 2 the isometry groups are amenable, which gives a finitely additive, isometry-invariant measure defined on all subsets, and that rules out any paradox. In dimension 3 and above, SO(n) contains free subgroups, so amenability fails and the paradox goes through.
