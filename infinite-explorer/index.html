Interactive notes on mathematical infinity

Infinite Explorer

Infinity is not a very big number. It is a different kind of thing, and it breaks your intuition in ways you can poke at. These pages are a hands-on tour.

Each page has a plain-language story and a few small interactive demos. Where there is real mathematics underneath, you'll find a collapsed Go deeper box with the more rigorous version. You can skip those and still follow along.

The series was started while watching Vsauce's video on the Banach–Tarski paradox, the claim that a ball can be cut into pieces and reassembled into two balls. That result sits at the end of a road paved with other infinities, so the pages are ordered to walk you down it.

1 · Counting

Hilbert's Hotel

A full hotel that always has room. Why ∞ + 1 = ∞, and how whole numbers, evens and fractions turn out to be the same size.

 2 · Bigger infinities

Cantor's Diagonal

Build a number that is missing from any list you hand me. Some infinities are strictly bigger than others.

 3 · The paradox

Banach–Tarski

One ball becomes two. Watch the trick at its core, a tree that is secretly two trees, and learn why it can't happen to a real orange.

 4 · Adding forever

Infinite Sums

0.999… = 1, a series that creeps to infinity, and one whose total you can rearrange to be anything you like.

 5 · Infinite geometry

Strange Shapes

The Cantor set, a curve that fills a square, and a horn you can fill with paint but never finish painting.

Suggested path

Go in order if you're new: pages 1 and 2 give you the vocabulary (countable vs. uncountable) that the Banach–Tarski page leans on. Pages 4 and 5 are independent playgrounds and can be read any time.

A few honest caveats

  • The demos are teaching toys. Screens are finite, so every picture shows the first few steps of a process that never ends.
  • Banach–Tarski can only be sketched here. The real proof needs the axiom of choice and some group theory; the demo shows the one idea that makes it all work.
  • If you want the full treatment, Stan Wagon's book The Banach–Tarski Paradox is the standard reference, and Wikipedia's articles are a decent next stop (linked at the bottom of each page).
