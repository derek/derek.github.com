Infinite Sums

What happens when you add up infinitely many numbers? Sometimes you get an ordinary answer, sometimes you get infinity, and sometimes the answer depends on the order you add in.

0.999… equals 1

It looks like it should be a tiny bit less than 1. It isn't. The infinite decimal 0.999… means the limit of 0.9, 0.99, 0.999, …. That list gets as close to 1 as you like, so the limit is 1. Drag the slider and watch the gap shrink.

The gap to 1

Gap from 1:

Whatever positive number you name, some number of nines gets the gap below it. A gap that is smaller than every positive number must be zero.

Another way to see it: if x = 0.999… then 10x = 9.999…, so 10x − x = 9, which means x = 1. That argument already assumes infinite decimals behave like numbers. The limit definition is what makes that legitimate.

Sums that settle, and sums that don't

Add 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 + …. Each step covers half the remaining distance to 1, so the total creeps up to exactly 1. We say the series converges to 1.

But adding smaller and smaller terms doesn't guarantee a finite answer. The harmonic series 1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + 1/4 + … has terms that shrink to zero, and it still grows without bound, just very slowly. Try the playground: pick a series, then slide the number of terms.

Series playground

The harmonic series is the shocking one. To get its partial sum past 100 you'd need about 1.5 × 1043 terms, which is more than any computer could ever add, and yet in the long run it does pass 100, and 1000, and everything else. By contrast the sum of 1/n² also has shrinking terms, but it settles down to π²/6 ≈ 1.6449. Whether a series converges depends on how fast the terms shrink.

Go deeper: definitions and the harmonic proof

The infinite sum Σ an is defined as the limit of its partial sums SN = a1 + … + aN as N → ∞, if that limit exists. If it doesn't, the series diverges.

The harmonic series diverges. Group the terms: 1 + 1/2 + (1/3 + 1/4) + (1/5 + … + 1/8) + …. Each bracket is at least 1/2 (the 2k terms in the kth bracket are each at least 1/2k+1), so the partial sums grow at least like 1 + k/2 and are unbounded. In fact SN ≈ ln N + 0.5772….

Geometric series: for |r| < 1, Σn≥0 rn = 1/(1 − r). With r = 1/10 and a factor 9/10 you get 0.999… = 9/10 · 1/(1 − 1/10) = 1.

The sum you can rearrange to be anything

The alternating harmonic series 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + … converges, to ln 2 ≈ 0.693. Now do something that is harmless for finite sums: change the order.

Take two positive terms, then one negative: 1 + 1/3 − 1/2 + 1/5 + 1/7 − 1/4 + …. Same numbers, every one used exactly once, and the total is now 1.5 × ln 2 ≈ 1.04. Rearranging an infinite sum changed its value.

It gets worse. Riemann's rearrangement theorem says you can reorder the terms to make the sum hit any number you like. The recipe: if you're below the target, add the next unused positive term. If you're above, add the next unused negative term. Pick a target:

Try a target between −6 and 6.

Every term of the original series appears exactly once, in a different order, and the totals are all different. This only happens for series like this one, whose positive terms alone add to infinity and whose negative terms alone add to minus infinity. Series where you could add the absolute values and still get a finite answer are immune: reorder them as you please.

What about 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + … = −1/12? Under the definition above, that series simply diverges, as you can see in the playground. The −1/12 comes from a different procedure (analytically continuing the Riemann zeta function, where ζ(−1) = −1/12), which is meaningful and shows up in physics, but it isn't "adding the numbers" in the sense used on this page.
