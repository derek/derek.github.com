The Twin Paradox

One twin stays on Earth. The other flies to a distant star and back at nearly the speed of light. When they meet again, the traveller is younger. But motion is relative, so why can't the traveller say the same about Earth?

The setup is symmetric only until you notice that one twin turns around. To reverse course, the traveller has to accelerate, and that breaks the symmetry. The Earth twin stays in a single inertial frame the whole time. The traveller uses two: one on the way out, a different one on the way back.

Scrub through the trip

Horizontal: distance from Earth. Vertical: time on Earth's clock. Blue: Earth. Gold: traveller. The pink dashed line is what the traveller counts as "now" at that moment, and where it hits the blue line is how old the traveller thinks Earth is.

Drag the traveller's clock through the turnaround and watch the dashed line. On the way out it tilts one way; on the way back it tilts the other. The point where it meets Earth's line jumps forward. Nothing strange happens to the Earth twin. The traveller just switched which events they call simultaneous, and that switch accounts for all the missing years.

So who's right? Both, about different things. They agree on what they see when they reunite: the traveller is younger. They disagree only about when things on the other side happened mid-trip, and relativity says that was never a fixed fact.

Go deeper: the numbers

With c = 1, speed v and distance d to the turnaround, the Lorentz factor is γ = 1/√(1−v²).

Earth's twin ages T = 2d/v. The traveller's proper time is the length of the bent worldline: τ = T/γ. Proper time along a path is largest for the straight one, so the stay-at-home twin ages the most.

On the way out, the traveller's "now" line meets Earth at τ/γ. On the way back it meets Earth at d/v + vd + s/γ, where s is the traveller's time since the turn. At the turn the jump is 2vd years. Add the slowed Earth clock on each leg to that jump and you recover exactly T.

The effect is real: atomic clocks flown on airliners (Hafele–Keating, 1971) and cosmic-ray muons that survive longer when moving fast both show it.
