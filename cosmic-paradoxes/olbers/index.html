Why Is the Night Dark?

If the universe is infinite, eternal and full of stars, then every line of sight ends on a star's surface, and the whole sky should be as bright as the Sun. It isn't. That simple observation says something deep about the universe.

Here is the argument, due to Olbers in 1823 (and noticed by Kepler two centuries earlier). Take a thin shell of stars at distance r. The number of stars in it grows like r². Each looks fainter, like 1/r². The two cancel, so every shell adds the same amount of light. Infinitely many shells means infinite light.

Fill the sky

Each dot is a star (this toy draws them all the same size). Farther out there are more stars to hide the gaps. Slide out the distance your telescope can see and watch the gaps close.

In this toy the sky goes from dark to nearly solid white as the slider moves right. The dark night sky means something in the argument's assumptions is false. Three suspects:

  • Dust absorbs the light. It doesn't work: the dust would heat up and glow until it was as bright as the stars.
  • The stars run out. A finite number of stars would do it, but telescopes keep finding more galaxies, and the universe seems to extend far beyond what we see.
  • The universe has a beginning. Light has had only about 13.8 billion years to reach us. Stars farther than that, in light-travel terms, are invisible because their light hasn't arrived yet. This is the main answer.

The surprise: a dark night sky is evidence for the Big Bang. Edgar Allan Poe guessed the answer in 1848, and it holds up. (Expansion helps a little too, by stretching the light to redder wavelengths, but the finite age does most of the work.)

Go deeper: how big is the effect?

Stars with number density n and radius R★ block a fraction of the sky given by the optical depth τ = nπR★² · r. The sky is covered when τ ≈ 1, which needs r ≈ 1/(nπR★²), the "mean free path".

For the real universe that distance comes out many orders of magnitude larger than the observable universe, which is only a few tens of billions of light-years across in radius. So the sky is dark by an enormous factor, not a close call: only a tiny sliver of it is "covered" by star disks within what we can see.

The slider in the demo uses a mean free path of about 30 units so you can see the effect. The shape of the curve, 1 − e^(−r/r₀), is the real one.
