The Fermi Paradox

The Milky Way has a few hundred billion stars and is over ten billion years old. Many of those stars have planets. Given that much time and room, someone should have turned up by now. So where is everybody?

Over lunch in 1950 the physicist Enrico Fermi reportedly asked just that. The question has no accepted answer, which makes this the one open paradox in this collection. The best tool for arguing about it is the Drake equation, which multiplies a chain of factors to estimate how many civilizations we could talk to right now.

The Drake equation

Notice how little the first three factors matter next to the last four. We now know that planets are common, so those have shrunk the uncertainty. The rest, how often does life start, how often does it become intelligent, how long do civilizations last, are guesses where one data point (us) is all we have. Their plausible ranges span many orders of magnitude, and the answer swings from "we are alone" to "the galaxy is crowded" depending on what you pick.

A sobering check: the demo's "chance of being alone" uses the expected count N. If N is small, say 0.001, the most likely situation is that we are the only ones in the galaxy, and no paradox is left. The paradox only bites if you insist N is large.

Go deeper: the equation and its uses

N = R★ · fp · ne · fl · fi · fc · L

Frank Drake wrote it in 1961 as an agenda for a meeting, not as a prediction. It counts civilizations whose signals are reaching us right now, which is why L (how long they signal) matters so much: a civilization that signals for 100 years in a 10-billion-year galaxy is easy to miss.

A 2018 paper by Sandberg, Drexler and Ord propagated realistic uncertainty ranges through the equation instead of using single numbers. Their conclusion was that a substantial chance of being alone in the galaxy is perfectly consistent with what we know. The "paradox" mostly comes from multiplying point guesses.
