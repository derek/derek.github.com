Faster Than Light, Sort Of

Nothing can outrun light. Yet beyond a certain distance, galaxies are receding from us faster than light. And we can still see some of them. Both statements are true.

The trick is that those galaxies aren't moving through space faster than light. Space itself is stretching between us and them. The speed limit applies to motion through space, not to the growth of space. The farther away a galaxy is, the more space lies between us and it, so the more is added per second. That is Hubble's law:

v = H₀ · d

Three boundaries, easy to mix up

BoundaryRoughlyWhat it means
Hubble sphere14 billion lyGalaxies beyond it recede faster than light right now.
Event horizon16–17 billion lyLight sent from beyond it today will never reach us.
Particle horizon46 billion lyThe edge of the observable universe: the farthest matter whose old light could have reached us.

Why can we see galaxies that are receding faster than light? Because the Hubble sphere itself grows. Light that set out toward us in a region receding faster than light is initially carried away, but the Hubble sphere eventually expands past it and the light starts to make headway. We see the galaxy as it was long ago, when it was much closer.

The most distant galaxies we can see today are now roughly 30+ billion light-years away, even though the universe is only 13.8 billion years old. No contradiction: the distance is "how far now", not "how far the light travelled".

Misreading to avoid: relativity says nothing can pass another object locally faster than light. It places no limit on how fast the distance between two far-apart things can grow.

Go deeper: the arithmetic

The Hubble distance is dH = c/H₀. With H₀ = 70 km/s/Mpc, dH = 299,792 / 70 ≈ 4,283 Mpc, about 13.97 billion light-years (1 Mpc ≈ 3.262 million ly). The recession speed in the demo is v/c = d/dH.

The horizon values in the table depend on the full model (dark energy, matter, radiation), so the demo only uses them as fixed markers for a standard ΛCDM universe, with the rounded numbers above. They would shift slightly with different parameters.

Cosmological redshift is not an ordinary Doppler shift, and a naive Doppler reading of "v > c" breaks down for the same reason that the Hubble-law speed does. Distances here are "proper distance now" in the cosmologists' sense.
