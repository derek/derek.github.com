The webmaster: a mailbox that became a job

For about a decade, a website had one person behind it and one address at the bottom of the page where you could reach them. The address has lasted far longer than the job.

Hi Webmaster!
  ...
Can you help?

Those are the greeting and the question from an email sent on March 16, 1995, under the subject "Error 404". The parts I left out are one sentence of explanation, a sign-off and a pasted error: "WWW Alert: Fatal Error 404 No Match Found for", followed by an address on CERN's server. The World Wide Web Consortium still keeps the message in a folder of its history archive called MailArchive/webmaster.

The folder is worth a visit. Its index covers three weeks, from March 14 to April 4, 1995, and lists 405 messages. That is about nineteen a day, arriving in a mailbox that existed because a style guide had suggested it. The subject lines include "help", "Need help!", "test", "Weather", "healing", "Search Frustrations" and a great many "(no subject)".

The word in that greeting became a job title, a magazine survey category, a line in an RFC, the name of a Google product and, eventually, something people stopped calling themselves. This essay follows the word and the address together, because they started as the same thing. "Webmaster" was a mailbox first and an occupation second.

Before: sign your initials

Email had worked out its own version of the problem years earlier. A mail system needs a human who can be told that the mail system is broken, and the address for that human cannot depend on knowing their name. So the name was fixed in advance. As a later RFC put it, the mail standards require "the presence of a <POSTMASTER@domain> mailbox name on all hosts that have an SMTP server."

The early web had no equivalent, and at first it did not think it needed one. W3C keeps a snapshot of CERN's web pages from November 3, 1992, and in it is a page of etiquette for people publishing on the new system. Its first rule is headed "Sign it!":

Make a page for yourself with your mail address and phone number. At the bottom of files for which you are responsible, put a small note -- say just your initials -- and link it to that page.

The page follows its own advice. Here is its last line, exactly as served:

<ADDRESS><A NAME=0 HREF=http://info.cern.ch./hypertext/TBL_Disclaimer.html>Tim BL</A>

That is the original footer contact link, and it points at a person. In 1992 responsibility on the web was per document. Each page had an author, the author had a phone book entry, and if something was wrong you wrote to whoever's initials were at the bottom. The same page tells you to give your server a root page for outsiders and a stable alias like www.dom.edu. It has no word for the person who runs the server, and no address for them either.

A word coined on a style page

The later version of that page, which W3C still serves with a "1992-95" credit at the bottom, has a new section. It is called "An alias for yourself":

You should make a mail alias "webmaster" on the server machine so that people who have problems with your server can mail you about it easily. This is similar to the "postmaster" alias for people who have mail problems with your machine.

And near the top, Tim Berners-Lee added a note that is rare among etymologies, in that the coiner is standing right there: "As a server administrator, or webmaster as they are known (the term having been coined on this page, below) you should make sure this applies to your data."

When exactly? The November 1992 snapshot does not have the section. The later page carries a remark dated "[3/94]". Merriam-Webster gives the first known use of "webmaster" as 1993. So my best guess is 1993, and I can't narrow it further than "after November 1992".

Look at what was actually defined. It was a mail alias, built by analogy with one that already existed, for "people who have problems with your server". It described who should receive complaints. Nothing in it said the recipient should also write the pages, draw the buttons or choose the colours.

In fact the same page argues against it. In the very next section, "Delegating control", Berners-Lee wrote: "Maybe the server administrator has no responsibility at all for the actual content of the data, in which case he or she should just keep the machine running properly." The person who coined the word separated server from content almost in the same breath. The web then spent a few years ignoring the distinction.

The address gets wired in

The other half of the footer link arrived in December 1994. RFC 1738, the URL specification that Berners-Lee co-edited, defined a mailto scheme "used to designate the Internet mailing address of an individual or service." Note the last two words. With the alias and the scheme together, any page could end with a link in this pattern (the domain here is the etiquette page's own example):

<A HREF="mailto:webmaster@dom.edu">

There was a more formal route too. HTML 3.2, published in January 1997, noted that "REV=made is sometimes used to identify the document author, either the author's email address with a mailto URL, or a link to the author's home page." Lynx did something with it. Its user guide still documents a c command to "send a mail message to the owner of the current document", which works when the page's head contains this:

<LINK REV="made" HREF="mailto:user@somedomain.com">

Then the servers picked the idea up. Apache's sample configuration, in the 1.3 branch, has these lines:

#
# ServerAdmin: Your address, where problems with the server should be
# e-mailed.  This address appears on some server-generated pages, such
# as error documents.
#
ServerAdmin you@your.address

That comment undersells it. The 1.3 source builds the Internal Server Error page from a fixed text with the address dropped into the middle: "Please contact the server administrator, [the address] and inform them of the time the error occurred, and anything you might have done that may have caused the error." So at the moment a site failed worst, the server printed the webmaster's email address and suggested the visitor consider their own part in it.

The last step was ratification. In May 1997 D. Crocker of the Internet Mail Consortium published RFC 2142, "Mailbox Names for Common Services, Roles and Functions". It did not claim to invent anything. "Other protocols have defacto standards for well known mailbox names," it says, "such as <USENET@domain> for NNTP (see [RFC977]), and <WEBMASTER@domain> for HTTP". Its table lists WEBMASTER against HTTP, with WWW as a synonym. It also insists that case must not matter, which produces the best sentence in the document: "POSTMASTER, postmaster, Postmaster, PostMaster, and even PoStMaStEr are to be treated the same".

A few years after a suggestion on a style page, the alias was on the standards track.

How an alias became a job

Meanwhile the name on the mailbox had become the name of the person reading it. An undated Princeton Review career profile of the webmaster cites a 1996 survey by Web Week magazine in which "35 percent of respondents reported their official title as 'Webmaster' (either exclusively or in addition to another title), while none reported having that title the previous year."

Jay Hoffmann, who writes The History of the Web, describes the role as it was understood by then: webmasters "were at once the architect and the builder of a website, responsible for every bit of code and content that came through it." Hoffmann's candidate for the first of them is Louise Addis, a librarian at the Stanford Linear Accelerator Center, who ran the day-to-day operation of the lab's early website. Why did one title swallow the whole site? I count four forces, and none of them was a mistake.

The sites were small. Hoffmann's description of a mid-1990s business site is a front door to the internet, "updated weekly, maybe even less". One person could keep several of those going.

The stack was shallow. A site was a directory of files, a server with one configuration file, and maybe a folder of CGI scripts. All of it fitted in one head. This is my own reading of the period's software, but the Apache file above is a fair exhibit: one setting for who gets told about problems, and it takes a single address.

The institutions were used to it. The web grew up in labs and universities. As Hoffmann puts it, these were places where "it was not uncommon for people to have these pet projects on top of their existing work". A librarian who also ran a web server was normal there.

Nobody knew what else to call it. Organizations wanted a website before they had any idea which department a website belonged to. A webmaster, in Hoffmann's words, "was brought in by a business or a client or an organization when they needed a website and they didn't know who else to call." Hiring one person with a new title was easier than deciding whether the thing was IT, marketing or publishing.

What the mailbox actually got

The alias was meant for "people who have problems with your server". The W3C archive shows what arrived instead, and it is the best job description of a webmaster I have found.

Some of it was exactly what the alias was for. The first message in the archive, from a university in Berlin, is a precise bug report. A geographical listing on W3C's server had grown to 1,118,838 bytes, and the connection gave out after nine minutes: "By that time, we have received the African, Asian and Australian sections and the rest of the world is cut off."

Much of it was not about the server at all. One writer was "desperately looking for a design guide for html documents." One, under the subject "Where I am in here", wrote: "I'm trying to figure out how to get into this network and not having much luck." One message, apart from a name and a signature block, says only: "Hello ! Greetings from Warsaw", from "a WWW user". To a newcomer, the webmaster of the web's home site was the front desk of the entire internet.

Software authors noticed the pattern. Apache's documentation for ServerAdmin still advises: "It may be worth setting up a dedicated address for this", and gives the reason with some feeling: "users do not always mention that they are talking about the server!"

Then came the mail nobody had asked for. RFC 2142 had seen it coming. Its entire security section is one sentence: "Denial of service attacks (flooding a mailbox with junk) will be easier after this document becomes a standard, since more systems will support the same set of mailbox names." An address that everyone can guess, printed in a link that any program can read, is a gift to anyone compiling a mailing list.

Webmasters coped in two ways. The first was to stop printing the address and let a script build it in the browser, on the theory that a harvesting program reads HTML and does not run JavaScript. This is a typical version, written to run as shown and not taken from any one site:

<script type="text/javascript">
var u = "webmaster";
var h = "example" + ".com";
document.write('<a href="mai' +
  'lto:' + u + '@' + h + '">' +
  u + '@' + h + '<\/a>');
</script>

Wikipedia's article on address munging records the trick and its limit: newer harvesting programs can often run the script themselves.

The second was to replace the link with a form. The standard tool was FormMail, a Perl script from Matt's Script Archive that turned any HTML form into an email. Its page calls it "quite possibily the most used CGI program on the internet, having been downloaded over 2,000,000 times since 1997." View the source of that page and you find a notice, now commented out, dated April 19, 2002: "Several important spam-related security holes have been fixed". The script installed to keep the webmaster out of spammers' lists had spam problems of its own.

Why the title wore out

Hoffmann gives two reasons the role faded, and I think both are right. First, in Hoffmann's words: "Things got too hard for one person to manage." Ambitious sites were now built by teams of art directors, editors, developers and writers, and Hoffmann says of one such site, Word magazine, that it was "well beyond the scope of what one person could reasonably accomplish on their own." Second, things got too easy: visual editors, content management systems and hosted services each took over one of the webmaster's chores, until, in Hoffmann's phrase, "one by one the jobs of the webmaster were peeled away and outsourced to some tool."

The title hung on for a long time at Google. Jens Oliver Meiert writes that on joining Google in 2008, "I joined the 'Webmaster Team,' and we were on Google's webmaster job ladder." The product for site owners was called Google Webmaster Tools until a post on May 20, 2015 announced that it would become Search Console. The announcement was polite about it: "It turns out that the traditional idea of the 'webmaster' reflects only some of you." The users, Google had found, were "hobbyists, small business owners, SEO experts, marketers, programmers, designers, app developers, and, of course, webmasters as well."

Five years later the courtesy was dropped. On November 11, 2020, Google Webmasters Central became Google Search Central, and the post said plainly that "the term is becoming archaic". A user study had shown that "very few web professionals identify themselves as webmasters anymore." The Princeton Review profile had allowed for it. Its outlook for the career warned that "what it means to be a Webmaster today may completely change over the next few years and may even become obsolete."

What the webmaster left behind

The address became a key. This is the part I did not expect. When a certificate authority issues a TLS certificate, it has to check that the applicant controls the domain. The CA/Browser Forum's Baseline Requirements list the permitted methods, and one of them, section 3.2.2.4.4, is to send a random value by email to an address made from "'admin', 'administrator', 'webmaster', 'hostmaster', or 'postmaster'" at the domain and wait for a reply. Whoever reads webmaster@ can be treated as the owner of the site.

That has gone wrong in the way you would predict. In March 2015 Microsoft disclosed an improperly issued certificate for the domain live.fi: "An email account was able to be registered for the live.fi domain using a privileged username, which was subsequently used to request an unauthorized certificate for that domain." The advisory does not say which username it was. Whichever it was, a list of mailbox names that began as a courtesy to people with problems had become a list of credentials. The same document now gives it an end date: the method "SHOULD NOT" be used from March 15, 2026, and from March 15, 2028 a certificate authority "MUST NOT rely on this method".

Every specialty rebuilt the mailbox. When the job split, each piece needed its own way to be reached. RFC 9116, from April 2022, defines security.txt, a file at a well-known path that says who to contact about a vulnerability. It cites RFC 2142, which also reserved a SECURITY@domain mailbox, and its complaint about that convention is that it "provides only a single, email-based channel of communication per domain". It even inherits the old worry, in a section headed "Spam and Spurious Reports". I'd argue security.txt is the 1993 alias, rebuilt for one of the webmaster's descendants.

The defaults still assume one person. Apache's sample virtual host in the current development tree still reads ServerAdmin webmaster@dummy-host.example.com, and the error text still asks you to contact "the server administrator" and describe "the actions you performed just before this error." The software still imagines a single reachable human who owns the machine. Production sites replaced that human with an on-call rotation, which is my comparison and not Apache's, and the visitor's half of the link disappeared in the trade.

The generalist keeps coming back. Meiert's post, written in 2026, argues that AI tools are "allowing a single person to once again manage the entire stack", and that the webmaster may be "leveling up" instead of dead. That is a forecast, and I'd treat it as one. But notice which half of the old role the argument revives. It is the breadth: one person across design, code and servers. The 1993 definition was the other half, the person who answers for the site. Meiert's piece does get to it: "the webmaster orchestrates and takes the responsibility".

The takeaway

The webmaster was an address before it was a job. Berners-Lee's page asked for a mailbox so that a stranger with a problem could reach someone responsible, and it said in the next section that this someone need not be the person who wrote the content. For a few years the sites were small enough that it was the same person anyway, and the industry mistook a coincidence of scale for a profession. When the sites grew, the profession came apart along the line the style page had already drawn.

The breadth went to "full-stack" and now, perhaps, to one person with good tools. The work went to teams. What mostly did not survive is the footer link's promise: that a visitor who found something broken could tell a human, in one click, and that the human could fix it. The address itself is still honoured by mail systems, sample configs and, until 2028, certificate authorities. It is the most durable thing the webmaster built, and on most sites nobody is reading it.

What I couldn't verify

  • That "webmaster" was coined on the etiquette page rests on Tim Berners-Lee's own note on that page. The 1993 date is my inference from a November 1992 snapshot without the word and Merriam-Webster's first-use year.
  • That the W3C folder named MailArchive/webmaster holds mail sent to the webmaster address is my reading of its name and of the greeting in one message. The archive itself does not say.
  • The 1996 Web Week survey is quoted from an undated Princeton Review career profile. I could not find the survey itself, and Georgia Tech's webmaster surveys from the same years would not load.
  • Louise Addis as the first webmaster, and the reasons the role faded, rest on one secondary source, Jay Hoffmann's history.
  • Google's 2008 "Webmaster Team" and webmaster job ladder rest on one person's blog post.
  • Microsoft's advisory on the live.fi certificate does not name the mailbox that was registered, so the essay does not either.
  • That address harvesting drove webmasters to scripts and forms is supported only by Wikipedia and by RFC 2142's warning. The FTC's 2002 study of harvesting would not load, so it is not cited.
  • The four forces behind the one-person job, and the comparison of the server administrator with an on-call rotation, are my own readings.
