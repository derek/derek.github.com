The conference that gave JavaScript developers a community was nearly cancelled for lack of interest, and by the end of its second day it had been promised to another continent.

JSConf 2009 is a conference devoted to the programming language we all love to hate and hate to love, JavaScript.

That is the first sentence of the About page on the JSConf 2009 site, which is still online with its early bird banner intact: $450 if you register by March 11, 2009. The pitch goes on in the same key. "Come to this conference if you love JavaScript, come if you hate it, come if you don't even know how to program." It describes JavaScript as "a language that often gets overlooked." As an advertisement for two days of your time, it is unusually willing to concede the other side's case.

The conference took place on April 24 and 25, 2009, at the Hotel Palomar in Rosslyn, Virginia, which the site's header rounds up to "Washington, DC USA." About 130 people came. Sixteen talks were on the main program, and a side room took whoever else had something to say. Before the year was out there was a JSConf in Berlin, and the name eventually went on events in more than a dozen countries. I'd argue the format of those two days, down to the tours for attendees' partners, is still roughly what a community tech conference looks like.

Ajax had a conference. JavaScript didn't.

It is not that web developers had nowhere to go before 2009. There was The Ajax Experience, with Ajaxian's Ben Galbraith and Dojo's Alex Russell on the bill, first in San Francisco on May 10 to 12, 2006 and then in Boston that October. The libraries had their own gatherings. In September 2007 John Resig announced "the first all-day jQuery mini-conference" for October 27 in Boston, and the next year's announcement reported that "over 100 jQuery developers" had turned up. That second jQuery Camp was scheduled for "the day before The Ajax Experience," which tells you where the centre of gravity was.

So you could attend a conference about a technique, or one about a library. The language underneath them belonged to nobody in particular. Jan Lehnardt, a CouchDB developer who was at the first JSConf, wrote in early May: "Prior to JSConf, there was no conference dedicated to JavaScript developers, heck, even the idea of a JavaScript community was hazy at best." The reason Lehnardt gave was that people wrote JavaScript part-time, "in addition to say, Python, PHP or Ruby."

Kevin Dangoor of Mozilla, writing on the plane home, was more careful about the claim, and I'd go with that version: "It was possibly even the first conference to feature JavaScript as a general scripting language in the same vein as Python or Ruby." Python and Ruby both had conferences run by their own communities. JavaScript ran in every browser and had no meeting of its own, and my best guess at the reason is Lehnardt's: most of the people writing it did not think of it as their language.

A deposit and a sold-out sign

The organizers were Chris Williams and Laura Williams, a married couple. Chris Williams had been to RubyFringe in 2008, a small Ruby conference in Toronto, and came home with an opinion. Lehnardt told the story in a 2015 talk on JSConf's history. Williams had "been to a lot of industry conferences," and having now seen one done right, concluded that a lot of them were not very good. Then Williams asked, over instant messenger, what Lehnardt thought about a JavaScript conference. Lehnardt said it was surely a good idea. The reply: "I'm glad you say this, he says, because I'm organising one."

The site's news page keeps the timeline. The dates and the hotel were announced on January 5, 2009, and registration opened on January 23. The About page promised that the organizers had "no ambition or desire to financially profit from it," and that whatever was not spent on "food, drinks, speakers, and, most importantly, you" would go to charity. The format would be "not barcamp-style and not multi-track style, but somewhere between the two." A speaker announcement in February signed off with "Be here or be static!"

Then the tickets did not sell. In Lehnardt's 2015 telling, a few months in, at three in the morning Berlin time, Williams said they might have to cancel. Lehnardt, trying to cheer up a friend while half asleep, offered: "if JSConf is a success I'll bring it to Europe." Williams remembers it the other way round. The jsconf.com account says "I made a deal with him one night that if we pulled this crazy experiment off, he would have to hold one in Europe." Both agree there was a deal.

A couple of weeks later, the story goes, it was time to tell the venue the event was off and ask for the deposit back, which, in Lehnardt's words, "as it turns out it is backed by their mortgage." So that nobody else would sign up and be let down, Williams changed the website to say sold out, closed ticket sales, and drove to the hotel. Near the end of the 45-minute drive there was an email asking how to get one more ticket. Then another. In total there were enough inquiries to pass break-even, and the Williamses, instead of cancelling, confirmed the conference on the spot.

Williams told a shorter version the following year, in a talk about how the conference got made. Dangoor's notes from it record the lesson: "conference tickets don't sell out until they sell out." People need to feel they are about to miss something. The same notes say Williams put 200 hours into the first JSConf.

Sixteen talks and a side room

The agenda opened on Friday at 9:15 with Francisco Tolmasky of 280 North on Objective-J and Cappuccino, and ended on Saturday afternoon with Peter Higgins on Dojo. In between came, among others, TaffyDB, Appcelerator's desktop apps, CouchDB, a server-side framework called Axiom Stack, XMPP in the browser, Joyent's hosted JavaScript platform, PhoneGap, Stoyan Stefanov of Yahoo on performance, jQuery UI and SproutCore. Resig had the last slot on Friday, listed as "Secret Topic," and used part of it to introduce TestSwarm.

Count the venues for the language in that list: the desktop, the phone, the server, the database. The site's front page promised you would "Write JavaScript for the iPhone, Android, and the Blackberry." Roughly half the program was still about the browser. The other half was about everywhere else, and that half is what the recaps dwell on. Brian LeRoux's PhoneGap talk, by the organizers' account, had a major hand in putting #jsconf on Twitter's trending topics.

The recaps read like people who had not expected to enjoy themselves this much. Stefanov called it "hands down, the best conference I've ever attended," and admitted to changing the slides "on the night before the presentation (and after the first night's party)." Dangoor noted "probably 50% fewer bullet points" than at other tech conferences, which is the kind of praise you can only give in 2009.

The side room turned out to matter as much as the ballroom. The agenda had promised "a side room for lightning talks and hackfests." By April the site was calling it Track B, "our free-form 'unconf' style track," with slots handed out by email, first come first served. Lehnardt reported that "the Track B room was packed both days." Stefanov thought there "wasn't a big difference" between speakers and attendees, "since attendees presented just as good content on the B-track as did the A-track."

One of the Track B talks was Dangoor's. Three months earlier, on January 29, Dangoor had published "What Server Side JavaScript needs", which argued that "JavaScript needs a standard way to include other modules," and started a mailing list called ServerJS. In the side room at JSConf, Dangoor showed what the post-conference write-up calls the group's "biggest milestone to date": one small module, math.js, loaded unchanged into five different server-side JavaScript environments. I don't have that demo file. These are the first two files of the sample code in the Modules/1.0 specification the group published, since superseded by 1.1, which also happens to start with a math.js:

// math.js
exports.add = function() {
    var sum = 0, i = 0, args = arguments, l = args.length;
    while (i < l) {
        sum += args[i++];
    }
    return sum;
};

// increment.js
var add = require('math').add;
exports.increment = function(val) {
    return add(val, 1);
};

The group was renamed CommonJS, and the spec's page lists Node.js among its implementations. require and exports were on show in the side room of a 130-person conference, months before Node.js had its turn on a JSConf stage.

Why it worked

The honest answer includes luck, as the sold-out sign shows. But some of it was structural, and each of these is arguable.

The language had left the browser. Lehnardt's write-up points at "the newly kindled research in speeding up JS runtimes" and at JavaScript having "a life on its own on the server." An Ajax conference was a conference about a browser technique. By 2009 the people doing the most interesting things with JavaScript were running it in places where the word Ajax meant nothing, and they had no event to go to.

Nobody owned it. A jQuery event was jQuery's, and a Dojo event was Dojo's. JavaScript had no company behind it and no foundation, so a conference named after the language was neutral ground. The sponsors page lists Mozilla, Yahoo, Joyent, the agency R/GA and Sun's startup programme. Sponsors' people spoke, but no one company's product was the subject of the event. Dojo, jQuery, SproutCore and Cappuccino shared a single track. My read is that this was the first time many of those people had been in a room where no library was the host.

It was small on purpose. The sponsor pitch offered access to "125 of the best and brightest developers in the world," and Dangoor reported the Palomar's rooms "filled almost to overflowing." With one main track and 130 people, by Lehnardt's account "most people met everyone else."

Hospitality was treated as content. Mozilla's sponsorship went to the food. Friday night was an open bar at the top of Union Station, courtesy of R/GA, and Saturday night was Joyent and Sun's. And there was a "significant other track": the April announcement said "Bring your significant others, we will be planning a track just for them" to see Washington while the conference was on. Laura Williams took spouses, partners and families touring Washington while the conference ran, so the evening events were, as Lehnardt put it, "nicely mixed." Lehnardt added a note to the rest of the industry: "Other conference organizers: DO TAKE NOTICE!"

The talks were given away. Every session was filmed, and the videos went up free on the conference site over the following months, one news item at a time. A conference of 130 could not have defined anything by itself. The recordings and a week of blog posts titled things like "the best conference you couldn't attend" did the rest.

The money was somebody's house. This is the force that is easy to leave out. A year later Williams wrote "Hug a Conference Organizer", which lays out the arithmetic: "roughly 55% of your budget" goes to the venue, a two-day event for 150 people needs "about $50,000" just to secure speakers, food and drink, and break-even without sponsors is "$189 per day per person." The organizer personally guarantees the hotel's room block. A community conference with no company behind it existed because two people were willing to carry that risk.

A franchise with two rules

At the closing announcements on day two, Williams asked Lehnardt to stand, repeated the three-in-the-morning promise to the room, and told the audience that if they thought the conference had been a success, they should let Jan know. Lehnardt's speaker notes record the reaction in a single word, which I'll leave on Speaker Deck.

Lehnardt had, the day before, met Holger Blank and Malte Ubl at the bar. The three of them ran JSConf EU in Berlin on November 7 and 8, 2009. The JSConf 2009 site announced it in August with a line Lehnardt was still repeating six years later: the first conference "proved to be the homecoming of a JavaScript community that didn't know it existed." In Berlin, Ryan Dahl presented a project called Node.js. Ubl's post with the video says "Ryan received standing ovations for his talk and he really deserved it!" That talk is its own story.

The second American JSConf, in April 2010, was pirate themed, "for reasons I still have never asked about," as Charlie Robbins said in a 2015 talk. Its schedule shows what the first year had taught. Track B is now a full column beside Track A, every slot reading "Open Slot. Register Now!" Douglas Crockford opened. Brendan Eich was smuggled in as a surprise. Ted Leung, attending for the first time, wrote that "the conference reminds me of PyCon in its early days," and that it had been put on by "a 10 person staff."

How the name spread is written down on jsconf.com. JSConf calls itself "a very loose federation of developers who share the same general idea about how a technical conference should be held." There are two rules for using the name. "You must have attended an existing JSConf event," and "You must have an existing JSConf organizer as oversight." Each event is run and financed locally. The overseer's main job, the page says, is to be the person outside the planning who can say "It will work out, don't worry," which is the job Lehnardt did in 2009. The same page tells most people not to bother: "most times it is better to run a 'JSConf like' event with a different name."

Plenty did both. The jsconf.com front page lists JSConf events from Budapest to Bengaluru, and a longer "family" that includes NodeConf, CascadiaJS and Web Rebels. By Lehnardt's 2015 count, more than 50 events with "well over 50000 attendees" traced their origin to that ballroom.

Chris and Laura Williams stepped back in 2015, after a farewell event that December called JSConf US Last Call. Its About page explains that after a serious illness in the family, JSConf US "will be on hold for the foreseeable future." JSConf EU called its 2019 event, "our 10th anniversary," the last edition "in its current form." The name outlived both. JSConf US did come back under that name; its 2019 site advertises Carlsbad, California that August. And in October 2025 JSConf North America was held in Maryland, "back under the OpenJS Foundation."

What it left behind

An identity, which is odder than it sounds. Lehnardt described the mood changing after lunch on the first day: "We are slowly starting to realise that we are JavaScript people who do Ruby on the side, Python on the side and PHP on the side." On that account, before 2009 "JavaScript developer" was mostly something a Rails or PHP developer also was. I can't measure that, but I'd argue the job title you see everywhere now needed a room in which people could try it on, and that the conference did less to serve a community than to convince one it existed.

The module system had its outing on the unscheduled track. Every require() in a Node codebase follows the CommonJS convention that was demonstrated in the side room in April 2009, months before Dahl's talk in Berlin. The work was done on a mailing list. The conference's part was to give it an audience, and it did that in the room nobody had curated. JSConf did not invent the open slot. Its own agenda credits the "-camp" style, and Lehnardt's verdict on Track B was "BarCamps rule." What it showed is why a curated conference should keep one: that is where things turn up that a program committee would not have picked, because in April nobody knew they mattered.

The name was governed like open source. The two rules are a fork policy. Anyone can copy the format, and the site encourages it, but the name is protected so that it keeps meaning something. By 2010 the conference's own site was writing it as "JSConf™." Open-source projects handle code and trademarks the same way. It fits that the North American event is now run under the OpenJS Foundation, whose project list includes Node.js. The conference and the runtime its European sibling introduced ended up under one roof.

Its code of conduct was forked too. The 2012 JSConf site carries a code of conduct with a paragraph that starts "JSConf is dedicated to providing a harassment-free conference experience for everyone," followed by a list of who everyone includes. The 2019 site claims JSConf "was one of the first conferences to implement and enforce" one. I can't rank who was first. I can say the text travelled: the page for React.js Conf in January 2015 says its code of conduct "is borrowed from JSConf Code of Conduct." The boilerplate at the bottom of a conference site today was once a decision an organizer had to make.

The fix became the next problem. The "sold out" notice saved the first JSConf, and the lesson Williams drew from it was that people sign up when they fear missing out. Robbins noted in 2015 that JSConf in 2010 "sold out, albeit a bit slower than it does today." Scarcity is what made the event feel like something. I'd argue it is also what made the better-known community conferences hard to get into for anyone not already on the inside. The later JSConf sites show the organizers pushing the other way: the 2019 one advertises scholarships, and the Last Call one a call for speakers "designed to spotlight the new faces and ideas in the community." My reading is that they were working against an effect their own first lesson had created.

The takeaway

The first JSConf was sixteen talks and a side room in a hotel across the river from Washington, attended by about 130 people, paid for at the organizers' personal risk, and saved by a "sold out" notice that was not yet true. What it produced was mostly social. People who used JavaScript on the side of some other job spent two days together and left describing themselves differently. The module convention, the European sequel and the stage Node.js was launched from all followed from that.

Its lasting invention was a way of running the thing: small, single-track with an open room beside it, generous to the point of feeding people properly and looking after their families, recorded and given away, and passed on to anyone who had attended one and could find an earlier organizer to tell them it would work out. Robbins put the principle on a slide in 2015 as "PEOPLE >>> SOFTWARE." Some of the software shown in that ballroom is still in use and a good deal of it is not. The format has outlasted most of it.
