Where did all the old web developers go?

Around 2008, nobody in the room was over 40. It took the industry about ten years to work out why.

var year = 2008;
var jsBorn = 1995;
year - jsBorn;  // 13

That subtraction is most of this essay. In 2008 the most experienced JavaScript developer on Earth had thirteen years of JavaScript, because that was all the JavaScript there had been. If you started your career with the language in 1995 at 22, you were 35. To be a 50-year-old JavaScript developer in 2008, you would have had to pick it up at 37, the year it shipped, and stay.

Nobody did the subtraction at the time. If you wrote JavaScript for a living in 2008 and looked around the office, you saw twenty-somethings, a few people in their thirties, and a manager. The conclusion most people drew was grim: web development chews people up, and by 40 you are promoted out, pushed out, or replaced by someone who knows the new framework. The industry then argued about that conclusion for a decade, in essays and in very long comment threads, before settling on the duller and more accurate one. The argument is worth replaying, partly because the same arithmetic has started running again.

2007: "Young people are just smarter"

The era's thesis statement came from a 22-year-old. At Y Combinator's Startup School at Stanford in March 2007, Mark Zuckerberg told the audience, as reported at the time: "I want to stress the importance of being young and technical." Then the line that outlived the talk: "Young people are just smarter. Why are most chess masters under 30?" And the explanation: "Young people just have simpler lives. We may not own a car. We may not have family. I only own a mattress."

The quote reached a Hacker News that was about a month old. The thread drew 21 comments. The first reply came from Paul Graham, who ran the site and was 42: "He had a point. He just phrased it in a way that alienated a lot of people." Graham's version was that young people have "a certain energy and willingness to see things in unconventional ways," and that math "is famously a young man's game." Others pushed back. One pointed out that Paul Erdős was prolific into old age. Another argued that older people bring a creativity that improves as you watch more ideas succeed and fail.

Look at what Zuckerberg's argument was actually made of. The headline was about intelligence, but the supporting evidence was a mattress. "Simpler lives" means no mortgage, no kids, and nowhere to be at six o'clock. That is a claim about who will work ninety-hour weeks for equity, and it describes a labor supply. Hold on to that distinction, because the next ten years of argument mostly lost it.

2008 to 2010: get a Plan B

By 2008 the phrase "it's a young man's game" had moved from mathematicians to programmers, and some people were already tired of it. In October 2008 a thirty-year-old asked the Joel on Software forum whether to get out of tech while still reasonably young. Matthew Heusser, a testing and agile writer, answered on the Creative Chaos blog under the title "On Being An 'Old Dude'": "'It's a young man's game' is something people should be saying at seventy-two, not thirty two." The evidence was, of all things, The Muppet Show. Its guest stars were established entertainers, and nearly all of them were in their thirties or older, because it took about ten years to do anything of substance and be recognized for it. "Those first ten years are the beginning of the story, not the end."

The essay that defined the anxiety arrived the next year: "Programmers: Before you turn 40, get a plan B," published on improvingsoftware.com in 2009. Its premise was that a programmer's employability falls off a cliff around 40, so you had better line up management, consulting or something else before you reach the edge.

The essay's afterlife says more than the essay. It was reposted to Hacker News in 2015, where it drew 351 points and 289 comments, and again in 2018. Each time, the people the essay was warning showed up in the comments. The top reply in 2015 came from a 46-year-old who wrote that the number didn't feel like a description of them. The top reply in 2018 came from a 50-year-old who reported being probably the best programmer they had ever been. An essay predicting that these people would be gone kept getting upvoted by them.

The numbers behind the cliff traced largely to one source. Norm Matloff, a computer science professor at UC Davis, wrote that "many programmers find that their employability starts to decline at about age 35" and that "statistics show that most software developers are out of the field by age 40." When the claim resurfaced in 2016, readers noticed that the statistics were never named.

In 2010 Vivek Wadhwa's TechCrunch piece "Silicon Valley's Dark Secret: It's All About Age" carried the ageism argument to a mainstream tech audience. The mood had shifted from "young people are smarter" to "the industry throws people away." Notice that both positions shared an assumption. Both took for granted that older developers had existed in large numbers and something had removed them.

2013 to 2017: where would they have come from?

The reframe arrived quietly, in a 2013 Hacker News thread about an ITworld article called "Older programmers are more knowledgeable, but harder to find." A commenter named nevinera wrote:

This article keeps asking 'where do they go?' The better question is 'where did they come from?' You don't see a ton of 55 year-old developers because they'd have been going to school in the late eighties, before most colleges had CS departments, and well before it was apparent that there would be permanent demand for an enormous fleet of us.

It is the opening subtraction, done with college instead of JavaScript. It got little attention. The anxiety kept climbing and peaked the following spring, when "Ask HN: What happens to older developers?" went up on March 10, 2014 and collected 554 points and 358 comments. The poster was in their early thirties and wanted to know what the middle and late stretch of a developer's career even looked like. Two months later Zuckerberg turned 30, and the internet took some pleasure in digging up the 2007 quote.

By 2016 and 2017 the demographic answer had become the common one, mostly because the supposedly missing developers kept turning up to give it. In a 2016 thread on an InfoWorld article titled "Where do all the old programmers go?", a 42-year-old described starting just before the internet took off, and said most people don't realize how few developers there were in the trade back then. Another 42-year-old reported that finding work had become easier approaching 40. That commenter's father had recently retired as a programmer after starting in the 1970s.

A 2017 thread on developers still coding after 40, 50 and 60 produced the tidiest statement of the math: "Programmers over 40 would have started programming before 1995. Programmer cohorts who started in 1985-1995 were likely much smaller than the web boom cohorts." The same thread took apart the statistic everyone had been quoting, that only about 13% of developers in Stack Overflow's survey were over 40. As one commenter put it, a Stack Overflow survey "measures the answers from the specific demographic of stackoverflow users who are inclined to answer surveys." And the first comment asked the question that should have ended the debate years earlier: would anyone think it odd for a 60-year-old physician to still be doctoring?

The age chart everyone had been reading as a funnel, with people falling out of the top, was a birth-year chart. The top was narrow because few people had been poured in at the bottom thirty years before.

What the cohort math doesn't explain

It would be neat to stop there and declare the whole panic a statistical illusion. It wasn't, and the same threads show why.

In a 2020 thread, a 45-year-old asked for age-friendly companies after a year of interviews that ended, as far as they could tell, in being considered too old. It drew 452 points and 445 comments. One reply was from a developer who had applied for "some 3¾ thousand jobs" between ages 42 and 46 and averaged less than one interview a year.

Both things were true at once, and they happened at different places. The cohort effect explains the empty chairs: most of the older developers people expected to see had never existed. Ageism explains what happened to some of the ones who did exist when they went looking for a new chair. The first is a fact about the 1980s. The second happened at the résumé screen.

What the argument got wrong

The Plan B was a ladder that hadn't been built. In 2009, the choices after senior engineer at most companies were to stay a senior engineer indefinitely or become a manager. The staff and principal track, where you keep writing code and keep getting promoted, existed at a few large companies and spread through the 2010s. My read is that a good share of the "everyone leaves by 40" pattern was people walking through the only door marked "up." Once a second door existed, a lot of them stopped leaving.

Zuckerberg's argument was about labor, and it aged the way labor arguments do. "Simpler lives" meant cheaper and more available. People with fifteen years of experience mostly did not lose the ability to do the work. They stopped agreeing to do it at midnight. An employer can call that a decline, but it's a negotiation, and negotiations move when the supply does.

"Keep up or die" confused two kinds of knowledge. The half-life of a framework is short. Nobody's résumé leans on Prototype.js anymore. But the developers who lasted were mostly the ones who had learned the layer underneath: the DOM, HTTP, caching, how a browser actually spends its time. That layer barely moved. The browser-forking mess of 1997 taught feature detection, and you still write feature detection. Treadmill knowledge expires and platform knowledge compounds, and the fear of 40 was mostly a fear that all of it was the first kind.

The field needed to get old before anyone could be old in it. Someone who started with the web in the mid-1990s is in their fifties now. Pave's compensation data, published this year, shows the average age of engineers rising across every major job family. The old web developer turned out to be a person who needed time to exist.

2026: the fear flips

Here is the part nobody in the 2014 thread would have guessed. The question "where are the old developers?" has been replaced by "where will the next ones come from?", and the group with the hiring problem is the young.

The same Pave analysis found that entry-level engineers fell from 22.9% of all software engineers to 16.7% by the third quarter of 2025, while senior individual contributors grew from 9.1% to 11.6%. CNBC reported in October, as summarized by Fullstack, that 71% of the growth in software development job postings between May 2025 and May 2026 came from senior roles, in a market still well below its pre-pandemic level.

The research on juniors points the same way. A Stanford study using ADP payroll data, as summarized by SoftwareSeni, found that workers aged 22 to 25 in the most AI-exposed occupations saw a 16% relative decline in employment, while workers aged 35 to 49 in the same occupations saw growth. An IZA discussion paper from June found a 14 to 15 percent relative decline in junior versus senior developer vacancies after ChatGPT's release. Its sharper finding is about where the change happened: employers were asking for more experience under the same job titles. In the authors' words, AI is "raising the bar for what counts as a qualified junior hire."

Some caution is due. Interest rates and pandemic over-hiring are tangled up in all of these numbers, and the most alarming figures in circulation come from the weakest sources. But the direction is consistent, and so is the explanation people in the industry give for it. Built In put it this way in September: companies aren't hiring senior engineers to ship faster. They're hiring them so that faster code doesn't turn into expensive cleanup. Code got cheap. Knowing which code shouldn't exist did not.

In 2009 the fear was that experience would stop being worth anything at 40. In 2026 experience is the scarce input. The scar tissue is now the product: knowing why the caching layer is strange, why that regression only shows up in Safari, why the rewrite will fail. If there is a 2026 version of the age cliff, I'd argue it has more to do with atrophy than with birthdays. The risk for someone 25 years in is letting an agent do so much of the work that the judgment they're being paid for goes soft.

And there's a bill attached. Octopus Deploy's 2026 AI Pulse Report, as reported in February, lays out three phases: experimentation in 2024 and 2025, a junior hiring freeze from 2025 to 2027, and a potential senior talent crisis between 2027 and 2030. Seniors are made out of juniors. Stop hiring one and, a decade on, you run short of the other.

The takeaway

The 2008 office had no one over 40 because of hiring decisions made, or not made, in the 1980s, when the field was small and nobody knew it would need an enormous fleet. The age structure of a profession is a slow recording of who it let in, played back decades later. Everyone looking at that recording in 2008 thought they were watching something happen in the present. They were looking at the past.

The same recording is being made right now. If the junior freeze holds for a few years, the developers of 2040 will look around the office and notice that hardly anyone is 38. Someone will write an essay about how the field burns people out in their thirties. The comments will argue about it for a decade. And the answer will be the one nevinera gave in 2013: ask where they would have come from.

The 2009 essay said to get a Plan B before 40. The advice that held up is closer to the opposite. Stay hands-on enough that your judgment stays honest, and teach someone younger, because there aren't many of them coming and you are how the next cohort gets made.
